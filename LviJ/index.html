W1 & Q2: Closed-loop scene consistency after 30 seconds (RotErr 55.6°, depth agreement 0.120), and agreement in identity and actions beyond position

Mat. Pix., Inliers, RotErr, and Depth evaluate the consistency of the scene across clients. The pairs of Table 4a are candidates selected offline from the recorded positions and camera directions. Autoregressive generation accumulates error, so the motion that a client generates from the key presses gradually departs from the recorded motion. In closed loop, the state model estimates the position from this generated video, so the shared state follows what the clients generate rather than the recording. The clients thus depart from the recording together, and the recording no longer indicates which clients look at the same place. To evaluate the consistency itself, we chose the pairs from the state shared by the clients in the closed-loop run: two clients that stand within 256 game units (eight body widths) of each other and each see at least half of what the other sees.

Agreement between two closed-loop clients that look at the same place. Each measure is rescaled so that 0 corresponds to unrelated frames and 1 to the recorded frames of the same players. Table R1 reports the unscaled values.

Scene state improves all four measures, and the gain is largest for the candidates whose recorded views overlap most (Tables 4a, 19).

Each client estimates the position of its player from the frames it generated, once per block, with an absolute estimate that limits the accumulated error. At 50–60 s, the position error is 2.64 body widths, against 11.42 for a MultiGen-style position update re-implemented in our framework.

Same player

A classifier trained only on recorded player crops reads the same player as rendered by two different clients. The agreement on generated video is close to that on the recording, both on the ten-second windows of Table 2 and over whole rounds in closed loop, where it is also reported by elapsed time.

W1 & Q3: Multi-client rollouts without position resets or memory clearing

A round lasts about two minutes, so no recording contains a long continuous session of the same players, and the submission concatenated consecutive recorded rounds. We have now joined the rounds into continuous sessions that require no reset. In the one-hour sessions, every client is driven by the recorded key presses of its player from held-out matches, all five or ten views are generated together in closed loop, and neither the positions nor the scene state is ever reset. The 15-minute videos use a continuous track built by joining recorded segments where the position, view direction, and velocity match, and are generated with recorded positions.

W2 & Q1: Synchronized FPS, action-to-display latency, and controls arriving during a block

In our evaluation, the clients advance in lockstep: before generating each one-second block, a client waits until it has received the states of all other clients for the previous block. Table 7 excludes these waits and reports 18.7–23.4 FPS per client. Lockstep is used only in evaluation, for reproducibility and for scoring against the recording; in deployment, a client uses the latest states it has received. With the waits included, the optimized client reaches 24.1–28.2 FPS with the full decoder of Table 7 and 30.7–38.9 FPS with a small display decoder. The optimizations are purely engineering and do not change the generated video.

The latency from a key press to its effect on the player's own screen is 0.73 s at the median with the small display decoder and 0.94–0.95 s with the full decoder of Table 7. Most of this latency comes from waiting for the next block, as controls are read once per block.

Optimized client

Engineering optimizations. The following optimizations do not change the generated video.

  • The session is warmed up before timing, and each client is pinned to its own CPU cores.
  • Clients exchange messages over direct connections instead of a shared file system, and a waiting client resumes as soon as a message arrives.
  • The depth map of a client is sent together with its memory entry, so the other clients do not recompute it.
  • Memory retrieval projects the cameras on four threads, and the matrix products are serialized so that the results remain identical.
  • Retrieval projects only the image tiles that can fill a pixel that is still missing.
  • A client reprojects its own frames on a background thread.
  • Latents received from other clients are cached instead of being read again.
  • The frames of a block are passed to the decoder before the remaining bookkeeping of the block finishes.
  • GPU work that requires only the client's own data starts as soon as the block is committed.

Display decoder. The small display decoder replaces the full decoder of Table 7 for display only, and the generated latents are identical. Measured against the recording of the same window, the frames of the two decoders are equally close to it.

Details

Controls are read once per one-second block, before it is generated (App. B). A key press during a block therefore takes effect in the next block: it waits 0.50 s at the median, and the first frame of that block follows within 0.2 s.

During a one-second block, the other clients extrapolate player j from its last published position and controls, so all of them use the same position for j. If the motion generated by j's client deviates from this extrapolation, j's client estimates the position from its own generated frames and publishes the corrected position, which all clients adopt from the next block. A discrepancy therefore lasts for at most one block.

W2: Network delay, jitter, loss, and stale states

We simulated network delay and message loss between the clients in closed loop. Rendered decreases by at most 0.07 and image quality is unaffected. When a message is late or lost, a client keeps using the most recent one received, so the other players are rendered where they were earlier.
